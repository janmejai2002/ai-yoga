# The Override Gap — Presentation Script

**Format:** 10 minutes · 5 content slides + cover · 5 speakers, about 2 minutes each
**Pace:** written for about 135 words per minute, a calm presenting speed. The full script runs about 9 min 40 s, which leaves around 20 s for clicks and hand-offs.
**Rule:** don't read the slide. The slide holds the claim (System 1: the audience gets it at a glance). Your voice adds the reasoning and the story (System 2). Each speaker ends with a question that the next slide answers, so the hand-offs feel like one argument.

## Speaker 1 — Janmejai · Cover → Slide 01 (≈2:00)

*[Cover on screen. Stand still and make eye contact. Don't rush the opening.]*

Good [morning], Professor. We're [Group name], and our topic is technology as an input to managerial judgment.

Picture this. A sales manager opens the CRM on a Monday. It says one of the biggest accounts is safe: low churn risk. And that same week, the manager books a visit to that client anyway. Why would someone with a perfectly good dashboard ignore it?

That space between what the system recommends and what the manager actually does is what we call **the override gap**. *[Point at the shaded wedge.]* It's what this project is about.

*[Click → Slide 01]*

Why does this matter now? B2B buying has moved online. Gartner expected most B2B sales interactions to be digital by 2025, so almost every decision today has a tool somewhere in it: a CRM, a pricing engine, a forecast, a scorecard.

But research doesn't agree on how people treat these tools. One stream shows people drop an algorithm the moment they see it make a mistake. Another shows people actually *prefer* algorithmic advice to a human's. Both come from controlled experiments. Neither tells us what happens inside a real organisation, with a boss, a budget and a deadline.

So we asked one question: when the system says one thing and experience says another, what do managers actually do, and why?

And note what we did *not* study. We didn't ask whether firms adopt AI. We studied judgment. We held [N] structured conversations across [N] industries, and for every story we pinned down the same six things: the context, the people, the trigger, the constraints, the options, and the outcome.

That gave us six decision episodes. [Name 2] will take you through them.

## Speaker 2 — [Name 2] · Slide 02 Evidence (≈2:00)

Thanks. One thing to hold on to: on this slide, **blue is always what the system said, and amber is what the manager did.** The story is in the gap between the two.

Six episodes, six sectors, from a distributor to a pharma buyer to an auto-component plant. Let me go deep on two, because they sit at opposite ends.

First, **E2, pharma sourcing.** The e-sourcing scorecard ranked the bids and said: give the whole order to the lowest bidder. The sourcing head didn't reject the system. The low bidder still got the larger share. But the incumbent kept thirty percent. Why? The trigger was a price-driven tender, but the constraint was regulation: if a new supplier's material fails an audit, a plant can stop. The saving was real, but nobody gets blamed for a split award. So the decision isn't human versus machine. It's a negotiated middle.

Now **E6**, the opposite end. A maintenance head got an IoT alert to replace a part early. Personally, they thought the part had life left. They replaced it anyway, because if the line stops, "the system flagged it" is the safest defence available.

Same kind of tool, opposite behaviour. Across all six, only one is a clean follow and none is a clean ignore. Mostly, managers *bargain* with the system. They split, they escalate, they follow it on normal days and drop it in the festive peak, they read three numbers out of dozens.

Every episode is written up in full in the report, with transcripts in the appendix.

So is there a pattern behind who follows and who overrides? [Name 3].

## Speaker 3 — [Name 3] · Slide 03 Patterns (≈2:00)

Thank you. When we laid the six episodes side by side, two questions explained almost every choice.

First, the vertical axis: *if this goes wrong, who carries the blame?* Second, the horizontal: *does the manager hold context the model simply cannot see?* That could be a relationship, a season, or a new person at the client.

Put them together and you get four logics.

Top-left, **Shield**: the model's view is good enough and the manager's neck is on the line, so they follow, because a system-backed decision is defensible. That's our maintenance head.

Bottom-right, **Override**: rich private knowledge, lower personal exposure, so experience wins. That's the sales manager who knew about the client's new buyer.

Top-right is the most interesting one, **Argue**: high stakes *and* private knowledge. Here managers neither obey nor ignore. They use the system's data as evidence to push a decision upward, like the deeper discount or the split award.

Bottom-left, **Filter**: routine calls, where attention simply goes wherever the boss looks.

Three things cut across this. One, trust is situational: the same logistics manager followed the optimiser for most of the year and overrode it in the festive season. Two, sector sets the default: regulated settings lean towards Shield, relationship-heavy ones towards Override and Argue.

Three, and this is our central insight: managers decide fast, on experience (System 1, in Kahneman's terms), and then call on the tool's slow, deliberate analysis (System 2) mainly to justify and defend. **The tool is System 2 on demand.**

So what does that mean for people who sell to these managers? [Name 4].

## Speaker 4 — [Name 4] · Slide 04 Implications (≈2:00)

Thanks. If decisions really run on these four logics, then a lot of B2B marketing today, which pitches tools on accuracy and features, is talking to the wrong part of the buyer. The real customer is the judgment behind the dashboard.

Here's what each logic implies, and each one traces back to an episode.

**Shield:** buyers follow tools that protect them. So if you sell analytics or AI into a regulated buyer, "ninety-something percent accurate" is weaker than "every decision leaves an audit trail you can show your boss". Sell defensibility.

**Override:** in E1 the account manager saw a churn risk the CRM missed. For a selling firm, that means relationship signals and field notes are data, not anecdotes. And if you build tools, give users an override that captures the reason, because that's how the model learns what it can't see.

**Argue:** in E3 the dashboard didn't decide anything; it was ammunition in an internal negotiation. Most B2B purchases have a champion who has to win an internal sale. Give that champion the evidence pack: the numbers finance and procurement will ask for.

**Filter:** in E5 the sales ops lead watched three metrics out of dozens. Content that doesn't connect to the few numbers the buyer's boss reviews is effectively invisible. Find those numbers first.

Notice that none of these says "replace judgment with technology". Each one is about designing offers, content and sales conversations around how judgment *actually uses* technology.

These implications are only as strong as our fieldwork, so [Name 5] will close with what was hard, and what we'd do differently.

## Speaker 5 — [Name 5] · Slide 05 Reflection + Close (≈1:45)

Thank you. We want to be honest about how we got these stories, because the method shaped what we found.

The hardest part was getting past the official version. Almost every manager first described the process as it is written: the scorecard, the approval matrix. The real episode only came out after the second or third "why". We also rarely saw the actual screens. Confidentiality meant we relied on memory, and every story was told after the outcome was known, which invites hindsight.

What worked was one change in our question. When we asked "What do you think of AI in decisions?", we got opinions. When we asked "Tell us about the last time you disagreed with the system", we got *decisions*. And using the same six-part template for every episode kept conversations on facts rather than views.

If we did this again, we'd do three things. Interview two people on the same decision, say the manager and their boss, to cross-check accounts. Actively look for overrides that went *wrong*, because our sample probably over-represents success stories; nobody volunteers their bad calls. And observe one decision live, rather than reconstruct it.

*[Pause. Slow down for the last two sentences.]*

So, to bring it together: across six episodes, technology rarely made the B2B decision. **What it did was shape what managers could defend.** Marketers who understand that difference will sell to the judgment, not just to the algorithm.

Thank you, we're happy to take questions.

## Likely professor questions (one-line answers)

- **"Isn't the 2×2 just a framework you imposed?"** No. The two axes are the two reasons respondents themselves gave most often ("who gets blamed" and "the system doesn't know X"). We built it after coding the episodes.
- **"Which logic is most common?"** In our sample, mixed outcomes (Argue, Conditional, Selective) outnumber clean follows or ignores. That's the "negotiated" finding.
- **"How do you know they're telling the truth?"** We don't fully know, and that's in our researcher's note. Hindsight and recall bias are real. Next time we'd triangulate with a second actor.
