SPEAKER 2 — [Name 2] (about 2 minutes). Thanks. One thing to hold on to: on this slide, blue is always what the system said, and amber is what the manager did. The story is in the gap between the two. Six episodes, six sectors, from a distributor to a pharma buyer to an auto-component plant. Let me go deep on two, because they sit at opposite ends. First, E2, pharma sourcing. The e-sourcing scorecard ranked the bids and said: give the whole order to the lowest bidder. The sourcing head didn’t reject the system. The low bidder still got the larger share. But the incumbent kept thirty percent. Why? The trigger was a price-driven tender, but the constraint was regulation: if a new supplier’s material fails an audit, a plant can stop. The saving was real, but nobody gets blamed for a split award. So the decision isn’t human versus machine. It’s a negotiated middle. Now E6, the opposite end. A maintenance head got an IoT alert to replace a part early. Personally, they thought the part had life left. They replaced it anyway, because if the line stops, “the system flagged it” is the safest defence available. Same kind of tool, opposite behaviour. Across all six, only one is a clean follow and none is a clean ignore. Mostly, managers bargain with the system. They split, they escalate, they follow it on normal days and drop it in the festive peak, they read three numbers out of dozens.
